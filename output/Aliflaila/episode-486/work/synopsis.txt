1. DETAILED SYNOPSIS
======================================================================

[Shot 1–7 | 0–66 s] [FRAME] Title card: Night Three. Darkness spreads, stars twinkle; deep silence falls over King Shahryar's great palace. The king is intensely curious, impatient to learn how the encounter between the terrifying jinn and the helpless merchant in the desert ends. Dunyazad, Shahrazad's younger sister, sits on a beautiful costly rug/mat beside the bed. As the last, darkest part of the night approaches she softly calls her sister as usual: "Dear sister, if you are not asleep, finish that wonderful story you stopped yesterday — tell us what happened when the giant jinn brought his sword down."

[Shot 8–12 | 66–107 s] [FRAME] Shahrazad looks at King Shahryar with her lovely eyes and very politely asks permission to continue. The king answers hastily: "Yes, tell it quickly! Did the jinn kill the merchant? What did the old men who had come there do?" Shahrazad straightens her hair and begins in a gentle, soft voice: "O mighty, wise king..."

[Shot 12–18 | 98–167 s] [TALE-1] Recap: the huge jinn had raised his great sword high, ready to bring it down on the merchant's neck. The merchant shut his eyes, ready for his last breath, heart nearly stopping with fear. Before the blade reached his neck, the FIRST OLD MAN — who was there leading a gazelle — stepped forward, raised his hand and shouted in a thunderous voice: "O mighty jinn! Stop! Don't bring down that sword!" The jinn froze and glared at him with blazing fiery eyes: "Who are you? How dare you interfere? This man killed my beloved son; spilling his blood is my right." (Back-reference to the earlier episode: the merchant unintentionally killed the jinn's son.)

[Shot 19–26 | 167–248 s] [TALE-1] The gazelle's owner, fearless, replies: "I know your son died at his hand, though unintentionally, and your wish for revenge is understandable. But I ask a small favour: there is a very wondrous story between me and this gazelle. If you find it more wondrous than what happened between this man and your son, will you forgive a third of his blood for my sake?" The jinn falls into thought — jinn/devils always love strange, wondrous stories. He lowers his great sword: "Very well, tell it. If it truly amazes me, I will forgive a third of his blood for you."

[Shot 27–29 | 248–277 s] [TALE-1 → TALE-2] The old man strokes the gazelle's head and begins telling the jinn, the trembling merchant and the others present. The gazelle he leads on a rope (ކަސްތަޅު, literally "chain/leash") is not an ordinary animal.

[Shot 29–39 | 266–370 s] [TALE-2] "This gazelle is my elder brother's daughter and also my own wife. We married very young and lived happily, but after thirty years God had not granted us a child; the sorrow of childlessness was always in my heart." So after thirty years he went to the market and bought a very beautiful young slave girl and made her his own (concubine). Soon, by God's mercy, she became pregnant and bore him a very beautiful son, lovely as the moon. His whole world filled with joy; he loved the boy and his mother deeply. But his wife (the gazelle) burned with a great fire of jealousy, hating the slave girl and the innocent child, hiding it from him though her heart was full of evil.

[Shot 39–46 | 362–433 s] [TALE-2] When the boy turned ten, the old man had to make a long trading journey to a distant country. Before leaving he entrusted the boy and his mother to his wife and charged her to care for them. The journey took one year. During that year the wife, to satisfy her jealousy, went to wicked sorcerers and learned black magic. Having mastered it, she turned the beautiful slave girl into a cow and the ten-year-old son into a calf. She called the husband's cattle-keeper (herdsman), handed the two animals over, claimed they were newly bought, and ordered him to keep them with the herd.

[Shot 47–52 | 433–484 s] [TALE-2] Returning home, the old man first asks for his son and the boy's mother. The wife, pretending great sorrow and weeping, says: "Soon after you left, the slave girl fell gravely ill and died suddenly; your son, crazed with grief, ran away from home and couldn't be found anywhere on the island." The man's heart breaks; he sobs and weeps, all joy gone, mourning his son for many months. A year passes.

[Shot 53–61 | 484–565 s] [TALE-2] The great Eid (Eid al-Adha) arrives. He orders the herdsman to bring the fattest, finest cow for the Eid sacrifice. The herdsman brings the cow that is really the enchanted slave girl — the man does not know. When he approaches with the knife (ކަނޑި — "sword/knife"), the cow looks at him and weeps loudly, big tears falling, rubbing her head against his feet as if pleading. Deeply moved, he drops the knife and cannot slaughter her; he tells the herdsman to take it away and bring another. But his wife comes, furious, shouting: "No! Slaughter it! It's the fattest, best cow we have; the best must be sacrificed for Eid." Pressured, he is forced to have the herdsman slaughter it; the poor creature dies with tears streaming from its eyes.

[Shot 62–65 | 565–600 s] [TALE-2] Wonder: when skinned, the cow had no meat and no fat at all — only bones; it had looked fat only through sorcery. Grieved and angry, he tells his wife: "Look what a wrong I did by listening to you! Throw its bones to the dogs. Tell the herdsman to bring a fat young calf."

[Shot 65–73 | 591–672 s] [TALE-2] The herdsman brings a small calf — it is his own beloved son! On seeing him, the calf breaks its tether, runs to him and wraps itself around his feet, weeping loudly, rubbing its head on his face and hands; its tears shatter his heart. He senses it is no ordinary animal but one begging for mercy in its own way. He orders: "Take this calf away, harm it in no way, slaughter something else instead!" The wife comes again, screaming louder: "No! Slaughter that one! There's nothing better!" She shouts for a long time, but this time, moved by pity and love for the calf, he refuses and orders the herdsman to take it and keep it safe.

[Shot 73–79 | 664–723 s] [TALE-2] Next day the herdsman secretly comes to him: "Master, I have happy news. My daughter is very skilled at breaking black magic. When I took home the calf you saved, she knew at once what it was: your own son, turned into a calf by your wife's sorcery. And the cow you slaughtered yesterday was your slave girl!" The old man feels as if the sky fell on his head; he weeps bitterly, grieving that his own hand caused his beloved slave girl to be slaughtered.

[Shot 80–87 | 723–787 s] [TALE-2] He hurries to the herdsman's house, meets the herdsman's daughter and begs her to save his son. She agrees on two conditions: (1) once restored to human form, his son marries her; (2) he permits her to punish his wife justly by enchanting her into an animal. He agrees at once. She puts some things into a bowl of water, recites words in an unknown tongue, blows on it, and splashes the water on the calf. The calf trembles and transforms into his beautiful son. He runs and embraces him, sobbing with joy.

[Shot 88–91 | 787–826 s] [TALE-2 → TALE-1] He marries his son to the herdsman's daughter. She then goes to his wife and, by sorcery, turns her into this gazelle. "O mighty jinn, this is the story between me and this gazelle. Is it not wondrous?" The jinn lowers his great sword to the ground, astonished: "Yes! Truly a wondrous story. For its excellence I forgive you a third of this merchant's blood."

[Shot 92–94 | 826–852 s] [TALE-1] The SECOND OLD MAN, who has two black dogs with him, steps forward: "O mighty jinn! The story between me and these two black dogs is even more wondrous. If I tell it and it is better, will you forgive another third of his blood for me?"

[Shot 95–104 | 852–934 s] [FRAME] As Shahrazad reaches this point, the golden light of dawn appears on the horizon and the call to Fajr prayer begins. She stops at once and falls politely silent. Shahryar, lost in the story, suddenly sits/stands up: "Then what? What is the story of the two black dogs? Did the jinn agree?" Shahrazad smiles gently: "Great king, it is dawn; my time is over. But if I am alive tomorrow night I will tell the second old man's story — far more beautiful and wondrous than the first." The king's curiosity burns stronger; he knows he can't bear not knowing the secret of the two black dogs. So he decides to spare Shahrazad again today, rises, puts on his royal robes and goes to the court (durbar) to govern. Night Three ends; Shahryar is slowly being bound by her wisdom and the magic of her stories. Final word "ނުނިމޭ" = "To be continued".

5. PLACE IN THE LARGER STORY
======================================================================
- Starts mid-tale: Night Three of the Thousand and One Nights; continues "The Merchant and the Jinn" from the previous episode (the jinn's sword was about to fall).
- Tells completely: "The First Sheikh's Story" (the Old Man and the Gazelle). The jinn grants one third of the merchant's blood.
- Ends on a cliffhanger: the Second Old Man with two black dogs offers his story for another third; dawn breaks; Shahryar spares Shahrazad another day. Next episode: The Second Sheikh's Story (two black dogs = his two brothers, in the classic). Third sheikh/mule not mentioned here.
