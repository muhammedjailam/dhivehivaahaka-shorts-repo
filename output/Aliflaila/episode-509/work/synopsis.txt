ALIFLAILA (އަލިފުލައިލާ) — EPISODE 509 — SYNOPSIS
76 shots, 0–686.7 s (~11.4 min). End of "Night 5" (ފަސްވަނަ ރޭ).

=====================================================================
1. DETAILED SYNOPSIS (in order)
=====================================================================

FRAME STORY = Shahrazad and King Shahryar in the royal bedchamber at night.
INNER TALE  = "The Merchant and the Jinni" (told by Shahrazad).
INNER-INNER TALE = "The Third Old Man (with the mule)" (told by the third old man to the jinni).
Nesting in the narration is marked with "' (double + single quotes).

[Shot 1–4 | 0–33.7 s] FRAME. Shahrazad looks up at King Shahryar with her beautiful, dark eyes, bows her head with great courtesy and asks permission to continue the tale. The king answers impatiently: "Tell it quickly!" He badly wants to know the secret of the third old man, and whether the jinni will kill the merchant — his heart is restless with suspense.

[Shot 5–7 | 33.7–60.5 s] Shahrazad begins in her soft, lovely voice ("O mighty, wise king!"). INNER TALE: after the second old man finished his story, the huge jinni had pardoned two-thirds of the merchant's life. Now the third old man, leading a MULE (ބަޣަލު) by the hand, steps forward, looks the frightening jinni straight in the eye and speaks firmly.

[Shot 8–11 | 60.5–102 s] INNER-INNER TALE (third old man speaking, 1st person). "This mule beside me is not an ordinary animal — it is my own wife." They married many years ago; outwardly she was beautiful and loving, but she secretly hid dark secrets — she was a wicked sorceress who had learned the worst kinds of black magic; he never knew. One year into the marriage he had to make a long trading voyage to a far country.

[Shot 12–14 | 102–124.6 s] Before leaving he entrusted the house to his wife and bid her a loving farewell. He sailed on a big ship; the journey lasted a whole year. Even in foreign lands he always thought of her.

[Shot 15–20 | 124.6–173.6 s] He returned at NIGHT (midnight, "މެންދަމު"). Wanting to surprise her, he told no one and slipped in through the small back door. The house was dark. He crept to the bedroom; the door was slightly open and a dim light shone inside. Looking in, his heart nearly stopped: his beloved wife was in his own bed with another man — a big, dark, frightening-looking man; they were "enjoying themselves". (ADULTERY — see section 4.)

[Shot 21–23 | 173.6–202.7 s] Shocked as if the sky fell on his head, trembling with rage and grief, he flung the door open and burst in. The lover jumped up, leapt out the window and fled. The wife showed no fear at all — she glared at her husband in fury.

[Shot 24–29 | 202.7–258.6 s] Her eyes seemed to blaze with fire. Without letting him speak, she scooped a handful of water from a water jar in the corner, recited a strange, terrifying spell over it, threw it on him and screamed: "Leave your human form and become a dog at once!" Agonising pain; his bones broke and reshaped; his mouth lengthened, hair sprouted all over, he fell onto four paws. Within moments he was a big BLACK DOG; when he tried to speak only barking came out.

[Shot 30–34 | 258.6–301.98 s] The wicked wife beat him with a stick, drove him out the door and threw him into the street. That night he roamed the streets in terror. Other dogs turned on him; three big fierce dogs attacked and he ran for his life, hiding among rubbish heaps behind houses. Hunger and cold nearly killed him.

[Shot 34–38 | 296–338.6 s] Next MORNING, starving, he wandered the street and found a fish shop kept by a kind old FISH-SELLER. He went up and looked at him pleadingly like a human begging. The old man saw the sorrow in his eyes, took pity and tossed him a piece of fish and a bone. The dog ate and, in gratitude, rubbed his head at the man's feet. When the seller closed the shop that day the dog followed him home and was let in.

[Shot 39–43 | 338.6–383.5 s] The fish-seller lived with his young DAUGHTER. On seeing the dog she suddenly covered her face and hid behind a curtain: "Father! Who have you brought into the house? That is no dog — that is a bewitched man! I know the science of magic; his wicked wife turned him into a dog by sorcery." The father was astonished and found it hard to believe, but the dog nodded his head and looked at them with tearful eyes to confirm it.

[Shot 44–46 | 383.5–409.9 s] The girl went inside, brought a cup/bowl of water, looked into it and recited beautiful words over it, then splashed it on him: "By the will of God, leave the form of a dog and become human again!" His body changed back; within seconds he was a man again.

[Shot 47–51 | 409.9–453.3 s] Overjoyed, he thanked the old man and his daughter again and again, then asked her: "O good girl, I want revenge for what my wicked wife did — teach me how to punish her as she deserves." Smiling, she poured water into a small flask/bottle, recited over it and blew on it, and gave it to him: "Take this flask home. When she is asleep, throw this water on her and command her to become whatever animal you wish."

[Shot 51–54 | 448–480.6 s] He hid the flask and secretly entered his house. It was MIDNIGHT; the wife lay fast asleep. He uncorked the flask, threw the water on her face and shouted: "Leave your human form and become a mule at once!" She woke screaming, but her voice changed, her body grew, bones swelled, and within moments she had become a MULE.

[Shot 55–56 | 480.6–497.9 s] Back to the desert scene (old man addressing the jinni): "O mighty jinni! This very mule here beside me is my wicked wife. Is this not more marvellous than the two other old men's tales?"

[Shot 56–61 | 494–550.8 s] INNER TALE resumes (desert). The huge, frightening jinni throws down his big SWORD and claps with joy; the sound echoes across the whole desert. "Yes! Yes!" — a truly wondrous tale full of lessons; all three old men's stories were the best he had ever heard. For the third old man's tale he pardons the last third of the merchant's life: the merchant is free to go wherever he likes. Then the jinni again turns into a huge black cloud of smoke, splits the ground and sinks into it, vanishing.

[Shot 61–64 | 545–583.6 s] The merchant feels reborn; weeping with joy he hugs the three old men and thanks them repeatedly: "You are the angels who saved my life!" The three old men go their way with their animals; the merchant returns happily to his beloved wife and children on his home island. "Here ends the tale of the Merchant and the Jinni."

[Shot 64–71 | 574.4–640 s] FRAME. Shahryar lets out a deep breath; his heart feels great relief. "That is truly a beautiful, wondrous story." Shahrazad, calm but confident: "O noble king, this tale is ended — but it is nothing compared to the next one I mean to tell, 'The Fisherman and the Jinni', many times more wondrous, frightening and exciting: the day a poor old fisherman cast his net into the sea and it caught a big GOLDEN FLASK/JAR, and the terrifying thing that came out of it — if you heard it you would be amazed." The king's eyes widen; a great fire of new curiosity is lit in his heart.

[Shot 72–76 | 640–686.7 s] FRAME. He longs to know what happened to the fisherman. At that very moment the beautiful golden light of DAWN appears in the east and the call to Fajr prayer (adhan) spreads across the capital. Shahrazad falls silent at once. The king tells himself: "I will not kill her today either. After hearing the fisherman's tale tonight, I will kill her tomorrow morning." He rises and goes to the court (darbar) to govern. Night 5 ends; thanks to Shahrazad's wisdom and storytelling skill her life is saved for one more day. Next comes Night 6, when the fisherman's great secret will be revealed.

2. CHARACTERS
=====================================================================

FRAME
- ޝަހްރަޒާދު SHAHRAZAD — female, young adult (~20). Storyteller, the king's wife. Beautiful dark ("ކަޅުކަންގަދަ") eyes, soft lovely voice, courteous, bows her head, calm but confident. Shots 1, 5 (start of narration; voice throughout 5–64), 64, 66–71, 73, 75–76.
- ޝަހްރިޔާރު ރަސްގެފާނު KING SHAHRYAR — male, ~35–45, the king. Impatient for the story, sighs with relief, eyes widen with curiosity; still intends to execute her "tomorrow". Rises at dawn and goes to the court. Shots 1–4, 64–65, 71–76.

INNER TALE (The Merchant and the Jinni)
- ޖިންނި THE JINNI ("ބިޔަ / ބިރުވެރި ޖިންނި" — huge, frightening jinni). Male, ageless giant. Carries a big sword (ކަނޑި) which he throws down; claps; voice echoes over the desert; turns into a big black cloud of smoke and sinks into the earth. Shots 6–7, 8 & 26 & 55 (addressed), 56–61.
- ވިޔަފާރިވެރިޔާ THE MERCHANT — male, middle-aged (~40s). Condemned man whose life was being "bought" in thirds by the old men's tales; freed, weeps, hugs the three old men, returns to wife and children on his island. Shots 4, 6, 59–63.
- THE FIRST & SECOND OLD MEN (ދެވަނަ މުސްކުޅިޔާ etc.) — elderly males; only mentioned (second finished his tale, shot 6; "the two earlier old men" shot 56; all three hugged/leave with their animals shots 62–63). Their animals are not specified in this episode.
- ތިންވަނަ މުސްކުޅިޔާ THE THIRD OLD MAN — male, elderly (60s–70s); also the narrator of the inner-inner tale (younger, ~30s, in flashback). Leads a mule by the hand; steady, firm. Former trader who sailed on a big ship. Shots 3, 7–56 (narrator), 62–63.

INNER-INNER TALE (Third Old Man's tale)
- THE WIFE / SORCERESS (މަގޭ އަނބިމީހާ; later the mule ބަޣަލު) — female, adult (~25–30 in flashback). Outwardly very beautiful and loving; secretly a wicked sorceress of black magic. Commits adultery, curses husband into a dog, beats him with a stick, throws him out. Later turned into a mule by him. Shots 8–12 (described), 18–31, 52–55 (as mule 54–55; the mule also stands beside the old man 7–8, 55).
- THE LOVER (އެހެން ފިރިހެނެއް) — male, adult; "big, dark, frightening-looking"; flees out the window. Shots 19–23 only. Keep as an anonymous shadowy silhouette.
- THE THIRD OLD MAN AS A DOG — big black dog (ބޮޑު ކަޅު ކުއްތާ). Shots 26–46.
- THREE FIERCE STREET DOGS — shot 32–33.
- މަސްވިއްކާ މުސްކުޅިޔާ THE OLD FISH-SELLER — male, elderly, very kind; runs a fish shop (ފިހާރަ); tosses fish and a bone to the dog; brings it home. Shots 35–43, 47.
- ޒުވާން އަންހެން ދަރިފުޅު THE FISH-SELLER'S YOUNG DAUGHTER — female, young (late teens/early 20s), good-hearted, knows "the science of magic" but uses it righteously ("by the will of God"); covers her face and hides behind a curtain when a strange man (the bewitched dog) enters — dress her modestly (hijab/veil). Recites over a water cup; gives a small flask (ފުޅި). Shots 39–51.

MENTIONED (next episode)
- ފަޤީރު މުސްކުޅި މަސްވެރިޔާ THE POOR OLD FISHERMAN — male, elderly; nets a big golden flask/jar from which a terrifying thing emerges. Shots 67–72 (mentioned only).
- Merchant's wife and children — mentioned shot 63.

Name variants: none significant. "މުސްކުޅިޔާ" (old man) vs "މުސްކުޅިން" (old men) plural. Note "މަސްވިއްކާ މީހާ" (fish-seller, inner-inner tale) vs "މަސްވެރިޔާ" (fisherman, next tale) — DIFFERENT characters, don't merge.

5. PLACE IN THE LARGER STORY
=====================================================================
- Starts mid-tale: continuation of Night 5 — Shahrazad resumes "The Merchant and the Jinni" right after the second old man's tale (two-thirds of the merchant's life already pardoned; the first and second old men's tales were in earlier episodes).
- Tells: the Third Old Man's tale (wife-sorceress, husband turned dog, fish-seller's daughter's cure, wife turned mule), then the conclusion of the Merchant and the Jinni (full pardon, jinni vanishes, merchant goes home).
- Ends: frame-story cliffhanger — Shahrazad teases "The Fisherman and the Jinni" (golden jar netted from the sea, terrifying thing inside); dawn/adhan, king spares her another day; Night 5 ends, Night 6 next.
- Note: in classic versions the third sheikh's animal is a mule and the transformation story is similar; here the details are this production's retelling — follow the transcript.
