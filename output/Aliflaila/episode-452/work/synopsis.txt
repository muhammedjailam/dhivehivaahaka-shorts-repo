ALIFLAILA (އަލިފުލައިލާ) — EPISODE 452 — STAGE-1 READER SYNOPSIS
124 shots, 0–1131 s (~18.9 min). Narration in Dhivehi (Thaana).

This is the OPENING of the whole saga: the full frame story (Shahryar, Shah Zaman, the two betrayals, the cruel marriage law, Shahrazad volunteering, the first night) plus the very start of Shahrazad's first inner tale, "The Merchant and the Jinni", which breaks off at a cliffhanger at dawn.

======================================================================
1. DETAILED SYNOPSIS
======================================================================

--- PART A: FRAME STORY — THE TWO KINGS ---

[Shot 1–4 | 0–44 s] Long ago, in the vast Sasanian empire (ސާސާނީ), reigned King Shahryar — powerful, just, beloved by his people. His rule stretched over wide deserts, green valleys and high mountains. He draws his sword only to protect his people's rights and defend the land. (Establishing: empire landscapes, wise benevolent king on throne, loving crowds.)

[Shot 5–7 | 44–71 s] In another part of the empire, in Samarkand, his younger brother Shah Zaman rules, also just and loved. For ten years the brothers have lived apart, separated by long roads and great deserts.

[Shot 8–11 | 71–106 s] After ten years Shahryar longs to see his brother. He sends his most trusted Grand Vizier with a great caravan — precious silks, fragrant incense, jewels, the finest horses — to invite Shah Zaman to his palace. Shah Zaman joyfully accepts.

[Shot 12–15 | 106–135 s] Shah Zaman pitches his royal pavilion/tent camp outside the city gate; the whole army is ready to travel. In the quiet of midnight he remembers a precious gift for his brother left in his chamber in the palace, and secretly, alone, rides back without telling anyone.

[Shot 16–21 | 135–187 s] By faint moonlight he enters his chamber and sees an unbearable sight: his beloved wife, for whom he would have given up the world, has betrayed him with a slave (adultery). Heartbroken, wordless and enraged, he draws his sword and kills both on the spot; blood flows across the white marble floor. (VIOLENCE + ADULTERY — see section 4.)

[Shot 21–25 | 179–228 s] He returns to the pavilion as if nothing happened and orders immediate departure. He travels in deep, silent grief. At Shahryar's capital he receives a warm, colourful welcome; the city holds feasts and games, but Shah Zaman's face shows utter despair. He wastes away, stops eating, falls ill; he cannot bring himself to tell his brother.

[Shot 26–28 | 228–252 s] Shahryar assumes his brother is homesick and arranges a great hunt to cheer him. Shah Zaman, too weak, excuses himself and stays in the palace.

[Shot 29–32 | 252–295 s] While the king is away hunting, Shah Zaman looks out of a window in the guest wing and sees a sight that changes his whole outlook: through a secret door into the beautiful palace garden comes Shahryar's queen, chief queen of the realm, with twenty maidservants and twenty male slaves; they begin to revel among the garden trees. At the queen's call, a large slave emerges from the trees and embraces her — the queen is unfaithful too. (ADULTERY.)

[Shot 33–36 | 295–333 s] Strangely, Shah Zaman feels relief. He tells himself: "If even my brother, a far mightier king, faces such humiliation and betrayal, this is not only my problem — it is an evil in the nature of all women." (Misogynistic conclusion that drives the plot.) His grief lifts, his health and appetite return.

[Shot 36–40 | 322–373 s] Shahryar returns, notices the change and asks why. Shah Zaman tries to keep it secret but, out of love and loyalty, finally reveals everything. Shahryar can't believe it; he wants to see with his own eyes. He announces another hunt, secretly returns to the palace and watches the garden. Just as his brother said, the queen and slaves come out and commit the wicked act.

[Shot 41–44 | 373–401 s] Shahryar's world shatters; his heart becomes harder than stone; love turns to hatred. He orders the queen, her maidservants and those slaves all killed. Their deaths don't quench the fire of hatred. A terrible madness grips his mind; he resolves never to trust any woman.

--- PART B: THE CRUEL LAW ---

[Shot 45–48 | 401–439 s] He passes a merciless law that makes the whole country weep: every night he marries a new young girl of the land, spends the night with her, and next morning orders the Grand Vizier to behead her — so no woman can ever betray him. Three years pass this way. Every day a girl trembling with fear is brought to the palace; every morning she falls prey to the executioner's sword.

[Shot 49–53 | 439–483 s] The joyful capital becomes a city of grief; parents' wailing echoes in the streets. Many families flee the country under cover of night. Soon girls grow scarce — only daughters of great viziers and emirs remain. The heavy, heart-breaking duty of finding the girls and carrying out the killings falls on the Grand Vizier, a good, righteous man whose soul suffers daily from the innocent blood "drying on his hands".

--- PART C: SHAHRAZAD ---

[Shot 54–58 | 483–528 s] The Grand Vizier has two daughters: elder Shahrazad and younger Dunyazad. Shahrazad is unique in the realm — beauty that could stop a galloping horse — but her greatest power is her sharp mind: she has studied history, tales of former kings, poetry and medicine; she owns a library of a thousand books and holds ten thousand stories in memory. She sees her people's suffering and the king's ruined soul.

[Shot 58–68 | 528–619 s] One night (interior, vizier's home, by a fire), the Vizier sits weeping over the innocent girl he must kill tomorrow. Shahrazad sits before him and, firmly, in a voice "clear as a silver bell", asks: "Take me to the king tomorrow; I will be his new wife." The Vizier recoils as if bitten by a venomous snake: "Have you gone mad? You ask me to lead my own flesh and blood to the slaughter!" She answers fearlessly (dark eyes without fear): she will succeed and save the daughters of the land, or die like the others — but she cannot stand idle while her people's blood is spilled. The Vizier pleads, shouts in anger, even threatens; she counters every objection. Finally, exhausted and weeping, he agrees and prays to God to save his daughter.

[Shot 69–75 | 619–681 s] Before going to the palace, Shahrazad takes Dunyazad aside, holding her trembling hand, and whispers the plan: after Shahrazad enters the king's chamber she will ask the king to bring Dunyazad for a final farewell; Dunyazad must sit by the bed, wait, and near the end of the night say: "Dear sister, if you are not asleep, tell us one of your wonderful stories to pass this long night." "The lives of everyone in this land hang on those words." Tearful, Dunyazad nods.

--- PART D: THE FIRST NIGHT IN THE PALACE ---

[Shot 75–83 | 677–752 s] That night the Vizier hands over "the most precious jewel of his life" to the tyrant on his throne. Seeing her, even Shahryar's stone heart flutters briefly at her beauty. Once they enter the royal chamber, Shahrazad suddenly weeps loudly. Annoyed, Shahryar asks what is wrong. Sobbing, she says she has a younger sister, Dunyazad, whom she loves dearly; knowing she will die at dawn, her heart breaks that she cannot see her once more — she begs that Dunyazad may sit with them in her final hours. Seeing no harm in a dying woman's last wish, Shahryar orders Dunyazad brought. The little girl sits on a soft, beautiful rug at the foot of the great bed.

[Shot 84–91 | 752–824 s] The night passes in silence and tension. The king lies awake staring at the ceiling, mind set on the morning's bloodshed. In the darkest hour, just before the first light of dawn, Dunyazad speaks in a trembling whisper: "Dear sister, if you are not asleep, tell us one of your wonderful stories…" Shahrazad sits up, long black hair falling over her shoulders, looks at the king: "Will the king permit me to tell a story?" Shahryar, bored of waiting for morning, nods: "Tell it." She takes a slow deep breath and begins: "O mighty and wise king!"

--- PART E: INNER TALE (narrated by Shahrazad) — THE MERCHANT AND THE JINNI ---

[Shot 92–98 | 824–889 s] INNER TALE. Long ago lived a very rich merchant with much goods and money who traded in far lands. One day, to collect a large sum owed to him, he rode his horse on a long, exhausting journey to a distant country. The sun made the ground hot as an oven. On the third day, worn out by heat, he stopped in the shade of a big tall tree beside a clear spring by the road, dismounted, washed his face and hands in the cool water, and sat to rest. From his bag he took a small piece of bread and a handful of dates, eating them one after another and carelessly tossing the date stones behind him among the trees.

[Shot 98–103 | 878–931 s] INNER TALE. As he finished the last date and threw the last stone into the grass behind him, the wind turned suddenly icy. Sand whirled in a violent wind; the ground split before him and a thick black smoke rose, swirling into a terrifying giant jinni — unimaginably frightening, eyes like blazing fire, long protruding teeth like a wild boar's, a huge sharp sword in its enormous, scaly(?) hand. The ground trembles at its step.

[Shot 104–113 | 931–1026 s] INNER TALE. The jinni roars like thunder: "Get up! Just as you killed my son, I will cut off your head!" The paralysed merchant falls to his knees weeping: "O mighty spirit, how could I kill your son? I don't know him, never saw him! I'm just a poor merchant resting in this tree's shade!" The jinni raises its sword (the blade gleaming with death): "When you sat eating dates and threw the stones behind you, my son was walking there; a stone struck his chest and its force killed him instantly. By the law of the unseen world, blood for blood!" The merchant swears it was unintentional, that he couldn't see him, begs mercy. "No mercy," cries the jinni, hellfire in its eyes, "you must die." It seizes the merchant by the throat, throws him to the ground, lifts its heavy sword ready to bring it down on the poor man's neck…

--- PART F: DAWN — CLIFFHANGER & REPRIEVE (frame) ---

[Shot 113–118 | 1013–1073 s] At that exact moment, the first golden rays of dawn slip through the thick silk curtains of the king's chamber; the beautiful call to the dawn (Fajr) prayer spreads from the capital's mosques. Shahrazad instantly stops and falls silent. Shahryar, eyes wide, suddenly sits up: "Then what happened? Tell me! Did the jinni cut off his head?" A gentle, polite smile crosses Shahrazad's lips: "O noble king, it is dawn. What I told is nothing compared to what is coming next — but sadly my time is up, so I must take the rest of this story with me to the grave."

[Shot 119–124 | 1073–1131 s] Shahryar's mind races; he desperately wants to know whether the merchant survives and how an ordinary man escapes a mighty desert jinni. He glances down at the executioner waiting in the courtyard below, then back at Shahrazad. To himself: "I'll spare her only today; after hearing the end tonight, I'll kill her tomorrow morning." He rises, puts on his royal robe and goes to the court (durbar) to govern for the day. That day the Grand Vizier sees his daughter still alive, astonished and overjoyed. "The first night has ended. But the real great game has only just begun." (Episode end.)

FRAME STORY
- ޝަހްރިޔާރު SHAHRYAR — King of the Sasanian empire, elder brother. Male, ~35–45. First just, kind, beloved; after queen's betrayal becomes a bitter tyrant with a "heart of stone". Wears royal robes (ރަސްމީ ހެދުންކޮޅު, shot 123); has a sword (shot 4). Shots 2–4, 7–11 (sender), 22–28, 36–49, 76–92, 115–123. Also mentioned 29, 30.
- ޝާހް ޒަމާން SHAH ZAMAN — King of Samarkand, Shahryar's younger brother. Male, ~30–40. Just king; heartbroken by wife's betrayal, kills her and her lover; wastes away, then recovers after seeing his brother's queen. Has a sharp sword; travels with army and royal pavilion. Shots 5–6, 11–38. (Spelling variant: ޝާހް ޒަމާންއަށް / ޝާހް ޒަމާންގެ — same name.)
- ބޮޑުވަޒީރު THE GRAND VIZIER (unnamed) — Shahryar's most trusted minister; father of Shahrazad and Dunyazad. Male, ~50–60, righteous, tormented by the executions he must carry out. Leads the gift caravan (shots 9–10); burdened with killings (46, 52–53); weeps by fire, argues with daughter, prays (59–68); hands her to king (76); joyful she lives (124).
- ޝަހްރަޒާދު SHAHRAZAD — Elder daughter of the Vizier. Female, ~18–22. Extraordinarily beautiful, but defined by intellect: history, kings' tales, poetry, medicine; 1000-book library, 10,000 stories memorised. Dark/"black-intense" eyes (shot 64), long black hair falling over shoulders (shot 89), voice clear as a silver bell (shot 60). Brave, calm, strategic; feigns weeping for Dunyazad. Shots 54–76, 78–81, 89–92 (begins tale), 92–118 (narrator voice), 121–124. Variants: ޝަހްރަޒާދު / ޝަހްރަޒާދާ / ޝަހްރަޒާދަކީ / ޝަހްރަޒާދުގެ (all same).
- ދުންޔާޒާދު DUNYAZAD — Younger daughter of the Vizier, Shahrazad's little sister. Female, child/young teen ~10–13 ("ކުޑަ ކުއްޖާ", shot 83). Trembling hands, tearful; sits on a rug at the foot of the bed and asks for the story. Shots 54, 69–75, 79–83, 87–88.

======================================================================
5. PLACE IN THE LARGER STORY
======================================================================
This is the beginning of the One Thousand and One Nights (Night 1). It opens the frame story from scratch (no mid-tale start): Shahryar & Shah Zaman, the betrayals, the murderous law, Shahrazad's volunteering and plan with Dunyazad, the first night. Shahrazad's first inner tale is "The Merchant and the Jinni" — only the opening is told (merchant, date stones, jinni demanding blood for blood). It ends on a double cliffhanger: the jinni's sword is raised over the merchant's neck when dawn breaks; Shahryar spares Shahrazad for one day to hear the ending. Closing line: "The first night has ended, but the real great game has only just begun." Next episode should continue the merchant's tale (traditionally: merchant asks for a year's grace, then three old men with a gazelle, dogs, mule arrive to tell stories).
