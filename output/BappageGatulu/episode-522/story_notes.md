# Bappage Gatulu — episode 522: story notes

Duration: 574.6 s (61 shots). Timeline: PRESENT throughout (the day after 521, then "the next afternoon").

## Synopsis
A clear new morning in busy Malé. Inside, Iyaan burns with a fire that will not go out. In front of the mirror he does
up his shirt and reminds himself that from today every smile, word and glance must be calculated — he is going to war
against one of the most powerful and evil men in the country. He goes to the Athena Art Gallery on Boduthakurufaanu
Magu, run by Raaya, Home Minister Asim's daughter. Behind the big glass door: the smell of canvas and paint, modern
paintings, few visitors. He stops before a large oil painting of a red sun rising from a black sea. Soft footsteps, a
young woman's gentle voice: "this painting doesn't show only a sunrise." It is Raaya, in a simple white dress, so
innocent it is hard to believe she is a killer's child. Iyaan: it looks like victory after a great war, or hope rising
out of darkness. Her eyes widen; she introduces herself as the painter; "I'm Iyaan" (narration: he offers his hand; she
greets him with a smile). Memories of his father's death come to him but his face shows nothing. They talk about art,
philosophy and famous artists — he spent nights studying everything she loves. She feels she has found an unusually
like-minded friend: the trap's first success.

Two cars stop at the door; security guards get out; Home Minister Asim walks in, white shirt, black trousers, proud and
powerful. "Oh, that's my father." Iyaan's heart pounds: the man who ordered his father's death is in front of him; his
fists tighten; it takes all his will to control his rage. A deep breath, a calm face. Asim takes Raaya by the shoulder,
looks Iyaan over with shrewd political eyes: "Who is this?" — "Bappa, this is Iyaan, he loves my paintings." —
"Iyaan... which family are you from?" Iyaan looks straight into his eyes: "An ordinary family, Minister. My father used
to run a business. He is no longer in this world." Asim nods — he does not see Ahmed Zahir in the young face — tells
Raaya he is off to a meeting and, turning to go, pats Iyaan's shoulder the way a superior does to an inferior. Iyaan
looks at the shoulder: "Your end will come by this very hand of mine." He tells Raaya he must go; "we'll meet again?" —
"Of course, I'll be waiting." Out on the street: the first trap laid perfectly.

The next afternoon, a busy breezy café by the sea near the Hulhumalé ferry terminal. Iyaan at a sea-facing table with a
black coffee and a notebook of names and plans; he shuts it and puts it away when Raaya approaches in a soft light-blue
dress, happier than yesterday. They order; she talks of her paintings and dreams while another part of his mind works on
a different plan. He casually steers to her father's security: she sighs — CCTV and bodyguards always around the house,
and the house can only be entered through a biometric lock system (Asim's or Raaya's fingerprint or face scan). He
sympathises — hard for an artist to be shut in like that; she feels he is the only one who understands her. Her father
wants her in politics, she doesn't; his office room is forbidden and always locked. Iyaan touches the coffee cup to his
lips: that secret office must hold the remaining evidence of his father's death and Asim's present dirty dealings — his
goal is to get in. He drives her home to Asim's castle-like mansion. They say they had a happy day. As she gets out, a
small key ring falls from her handbag: gallery keys and a small electronic card. He quickly picks it up; a tiny digital
scanner hidden in his palm copies the card. "Here are your keys." "Thanks, Iyaan." She walks to the house; he drives off
smiling: he now holds a copy of the card for the first security door of Asim's house. To be continued.
