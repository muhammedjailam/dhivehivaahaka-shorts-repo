# Emme Fahu Message (އެންމެ ފަހު މެސެޖް — "The Last Message") — series bible

Dhivehi audio drama, intimate grief/romance drama in four episodes that form one complete arc:
**326 → 327 → 328 → 329** (329 is the ending). Every episode's cover (`work/episode-<N>-cover.png`) is the same: an old
black rotary payphone with its handset on a coiled cord, on a vermilion-red wall, with the white Thaana title. Match its
mood (see `style.txt`): quiet, tender, melancholic; rainy grey-blue city light outside, warm amber lamps inside, with
small vermilion-red accents (a mug, a cushion, an umbrella, the glow of a phone).

SETTING: the narration is a translated story (coats, bicycles, cold mornings, a honeymoon in Italy). Set everything in
modern **Hulhumalé / Malé during the rainy south-west monsoon**: a small, slightly old third-floor apartment (broken lift,
a dripping tap, a tiny kitchen, a sitting room with a sofa, a balcony door, framed photos on the walls), wet streets,
grey skies, heavy rain. "Cold" = rainy, windy, grey. People wear light rain jackets, not winter coats. Honeymoon photos
in frames are small and blurry (a canal city with gondola-like boats, a beach sunset) — no readable text.

## Episodes
- **326** — Morning. Every day the first sound Amaan hears is not her alarm but Eethan: his slippers on the wooden floor,
  kitchen cupboards, him humming while making tea. Nine years together. Warm golden morning light through the curtains,
  dust motes, a slow ceiling fan, smell of tea. Small third-floor apartment in a year-old building, lift broken for a
  year, dripping tap, tiny kitchen where they bump into each other. Walls full of photos of ten years together: road
  trips, beach sunsets, honeymoon in Italy (Venice: Eethan pretending to be scared of pigeons, Amaan laughing), birthday
  dinners. Amaan reaches for her phone, groans at another cold (rainy) day, gets up, walks into the sitting room. Eethan
  at the stove in grey joggers and a faded university t-shirt, a frying pan in one hand, phone in the other; smell of
  butter. "Morning, my queen. Burnt the pancakes again?" Banter about the smoke alarm ("I disabled it"). Amaan hugs him
  from behind (SHOW INSTEAD: she stands close beside him at the stove, her hand resting on his forearm, both smiling at
  the pan). Teasing: "You're a 32-year-old woman." "I love you." He touches her temple with his thumb (show: they look
  into each other's eyes, smiling, side by side). The kettle shrieks; she steps away. 8 o'clock: breakfast table with two
  cups of tea, a lopsided tower of pancakes, strawberries, maple syrup that Amaan always pours too much ("that's soup",
  "that's diabetes" — she makes a face). Outside: people waking up, cars on rain-wet streets, cyclists/motorbikes,
  people in raincoats with headphones. Amaan's phone on the table vibrates: a reminder "10:30 am Dr. Mathews" (show only
  a glowing phone, no text). Eethan notices, asks nothing, reaches across the table and holds her hand — a silent promise
  that they will face today together. Her smile isn't complete. Afternoon: the fertility clinic — cream walls, rows of
  chairs, a TV on the wall showing a cooking show nobody watches, coffee + antiseptic smell; a place meant to renew hope
  that slowly became one that kills it. Amaan sits beside Eethan turning her silver wedding ring (seven years of tests,
  ultrasounds, hormone injections). Opposite: a woman flipping a baby-names book with her husband, smiling; by the window
  a pregnant woman with her hand on her belly, her husband leaning to whisper and she laughs. Amaan looks away, ashamed
  of her envy. "Eethan and Amaan?" — the nurse. Eethan stands first and offers his hand; she takes it and stands. Dr.
  Mathews greets them with the kind smile of five years — but today it looks sadder. "Please sit." Amaan already knows:
  doctors' voices soften before bad news; the room feels smaller. "I know how patiently you both waited for this cycle."
  He rests his hand on the desk. Her heart races. "We got the test results this morning." Silence. "I'm very sorry."
- **327** — "The result is negative." Amaan shows nothing, only stares at the doctor. Negative — the word that empties
  her every time. Dr. Mathews explains hormone levels, percentages, options, statistics — numbers, always numbers,
  none answering "why us?" Eethan asks calm practical questions (another cycle? what do you recommend?); she admires him
  for still looking for another door. The drive home is silent: wipers, rain sliding down the passenger window, the city
  a grey blur. At a red light he reaches across and rests his hand on hers; she squeezes his fingers. That evening the
  apartment is unusually, heavily silent; the kitchen clock ticks loud. Outside the sky darkens, rain clouds gather over
  the city; the first drops hit the sitting-room window. Amaan at the kitchen sink scrubbing one spot on a coffee mug for
  five minutes; behind her Eethan unpacks groceries (fruit, vegetables into the fridge, tins into cupboards, a loaf of
  bread on the counter). "I was thinking… maybe this weekend we go out of the city, just us two, to the sea, a little
  cabin you'd like" (in the Maldives: a quiet guesthouse on a local island). "I don't want to go." "That's fine." "I don't
  want to do anything." He sees she is exhausted — not physically, emotionally. "Amaan…" "I'm fine." "You don't have to
  be fine." She laughs a broken laugh. "I'm NOT fine." Five years of needles, medicines on schedule, marks on her arms
  (show: a pill organiser and a box of medicine on the counter, NOT needles or bruises); hearing pregnancy news from
  people who don't even try; "you don't know what it feels like when your own body fails you." Tears. Every month she
  tells herself this time will be different… she can't finish. He reaches for her hand; she pulls it away. "I'm tired of
  hoping. I think we should stop. The treatments." "If that's what you want, OK, I'll support whatever you decide." She
  flares: "Just like that? You don't even want to fight for it?" "I'm choosing YOU. Kids or no kids, I don't care." "I
  care! I always cared." "Then you're fine." "No, I'm not fine. My heart is broken. I wanted to teach our son to ride a
  bicycle. I wanted to dance with you at our daughter's wedding. But I wanted it WITH you. If that dream costs me you,
  it's worth nothing." For a fragile moment she wants to step into his arms — but years of guilt win: "You're saying it
  to comfort me. One day you'll wake up and know you married the wrong woman. You deserve someone who can give you a real
  family." The words hang forever. His face changes — she questioned his love. "Do you really think that of me?" "I think
  I ruined your life." He closes his eyes: "You never ruined a single day of my life." No answer. "I'm going for a drive."
  He takes the car keys from the table by the door. "Eethan…" "I just need some fresh air." A tiny smile. She wants to
  stop him, to apologise — she stays frozen. Hand on the door handle: "I love you." Silence — pride swallowed her words.
  He waits a few more seconds, opens the door; it clicks shut behind him. The apartment feels huge. Amaan slides down to
  the kitchen floor (show: sitting on the kitchen floor against the cabinets, knees drawn up, face in her hands). "I
  didn't mean it…" Her phone on the counter vibrates: Eethan calling. She watches until it stops. Again. She wipes her
  tears: "I can't." She declines it. Silence. A minute later the screen lights again — not a call: a notification,
  "1 voice message from Eethan" (show only a glowing phone face-up on the counter, no text). Her thumb hovers; pride; she
  turns the phone face down: "I'll listen later."
- **328** — Amaan still sitting on the kitchen floor, she doesn't know how long. Rain heavier, distant thunder. Phone on
  the counter, face down. She wonders what the message says ("sorry, let's talk at home, I love you") — not ready. She
  rubs her swollen eyes, gets up, knees aching, wanders the apartment: the blanket they shared on the sofa watching a film
  last night, Eethan's coffee mug on the sofa arm, his novel and reading glasses on the coffee table; she picks up the
  glasses ("they'll get scratched here"), smiles faintly, puts them back exactly as they were. 10 pm. She opens the chat,
  types "I'm sorry" and sends; a single grey tick. No reply. "Please come home soon." Not even delivered. A knot in her
  stomach: still driving, battery died, stopped somewhere to think. She scrolls up through thousands of messages, photos,
  shopping lists, heart emojis — "miss you", "bring a carton of milk", "beautiful sunset today", "love you" — smiling
  through tears; she touches his contact photo (honeymoon beach photo — show a small glowing phone screen with a tiny
  blurred beach-sunset picture, no text). 11:30, still not home; maybe she overreacted; he always needs time after a
  fight, but he always comes home. She goes to bed, lamp on; his side empty; his pillow smells of his cologne; she hugs it
  ("I want to apologise properly, I'll fix everything"). She closes her eyes — then loud knocking on the apartment door;
  12:47 am on the bedroom clock (analog, no numerals); louder knocking. "Eethan?" Relief — he's back! She hurries out,
  straightening her hijab (the narration says "hair"). "Eethan, I'm sorry, I was so upset I couldn't let go…" No answer.
  She unlocks the door — not Eethan: two uniformed police officers. Still pouring rain; the building entrance is wet.
  "Eethan's wife?" The senior officer takes off his cap; his face alone stops her heart. "Yes…" "I'm Officer Raain. May we
  come in?" Confusion turns to fear. "Is Eethan OK?" They step in, close the door gently. "I'm very sorry to tell you…
  your husband was in a serious accident tonight." Every sound stops — the rain, the clock, their breathing. "No." "The
  rain was heavy; he lost control of the car." "No… he just went for a drive. He's coming home." "I'm so sorry." The young
  officer looks down. She searches their faces for a mistake… silence. "No… NO!" Her knees give way, she sinks toward the
  floor (show: Amaan sinking to her knees on the floor, both officers a step away, one leaning forward with an open hand
  half-raised — NO touching); she can't breathe; "I didn't even… I didn't say goodbye… I didn't tell him I love him." Her
  sobs fill the apartment that this morning was full of laughter, where he kissed her forehead every day before leaving
  (show only as a memory: Eethan smiling at the door). An hour later the officers leave as her elder sister Luha arrives;
  Amaan curled on the sofa, cried out. Luha gives her a glass of water; she doesn't drink. Her phone vibrates: a medicine
  reminder; under it the notification still waiting, received at 7:18. Her thumb stops over it; she slowly turns the
  screen off: "If I hear his voice… I won't survive it." If she hears it, he's really gone. — One week later: the
  apartment overflows with flowers from family and friends, white and pale green (Eethan's two favourite colours), their
  scent in the curtains and cushions; on the dining table, by the TV, by the window where he drank his morning coffee.
  "Have you eaten?" — Luha. Amaan shakes her head. "Try something." "Later." Luha sits beside her and holds her hand; long
  silence. "I need to take Eethan's suit" (the narration: for the funeral — show only: Amaan at his wardrobe, NOT a
  funeral). "You don't have to do it tonight." "I know." She stands anyway. His wardrobe exactly as he kept it: shirts by
  colour, jackets on one side, a half he made for her that she never used; smell of his cologne and clean laundry. She
  takes the navy suit he wore to her sister's wedding. Memory (dissolve): that morning Eethan at the mirror fixing his tie
  ("it's crooked" — "it's the fashion now"); she straightened it (show: Eethan at the mirror adjusting his tie, Amaan in
  the doorway laughing, NO kiss); "Now?" he smiles. An ordinary memory — but the pain is bigger than his funeral. She
  presses the suit jacket to her chest; she wants to take it to him, see him in it one more time; reality returns, she
  sinks to the floor. Later Luha finds her still sitting there holding the suit; doesn't try to take it. That night she
  sleeps on his side of the bed (show: sitting up against the headboard on his side, hugging his pillow, rain on the
  window, phone on the bedside table). Every day since the accident she sees the notification "1 new voice message from
  Eethan." Her thumb hovers… she imagines his voice… something completely new… she can't. She turns the phone face down:
  "Tomorrow." For the first time in nine years she falls asleep without his "good night" (show: dark bedroom, rain-streaked
  window, the phone face down on the bedside table, a faint glow).
- **329** — Rain since past midnight wakes her; grief in her chest; she stares at the ceiling, turns to his side; his
  pillow still dented from her hugging it. Her eyes land on the phone. For the first time since he left she wants to
  hear it; she picks up the phone: "Just once. I'll listen just once." She plays it. The soft hum of a car driving, then
  his deep painful breath. "Hi love…" It's Eethan. VOICE MESSAGE (imagined: Eethan alone in his car at night, wipers,
  rain, dashboard glow, phone held to his ear or on speaker — calm, no danger): "I know you're still angry with me. So
  sorry I walked out without waiting… I didn't know what to say without making it worse. I wanted to call again but
  thought you needed a moment. But I wanted to say something before I come home: you are not hopeless, you are not a
  failure. Not having your own child doesn't make you any less a woman. I didn't marry you hoping for children. I married
  you for YOU — for stealing the blanket at night and blaming me, for crying when you see a cat on the street, for
  expecting me to eat the world's worst pancakes… for being the one who takes away a whole day's tiredness when I come
  home. I know you think I'm angry because we can't have a baby. I dreamed of our child too — little feet running through
  the house, toys everywhere, waking us at 3 am. But none of it matters more than you. Even if we never have a child, I'll
  be the luckiest man in the world, because I get to spend my whole life with you. You're perfect. I love you endlessly.
  I'm coming home very soon. We'll talk properly, and whatever we decide, we decide together; you never have to carry
  anything alone. And if one day we meet a little miracle, tell that child for me — even without knowing its name — that
  its father loved its mother endlessly. I'll always choose you. Every time. In every life. Coming home soon. Try to
  sleep. Don't forget to eat. Don't bake pancakes without me. We both know you need someone to look after you." The
  recording ends. She plays it again and again; sometimes crying, sometimes laughing at the pancakes, sometimes stopping
  when he says her name. The first days she carries the phone everywhere: folding laundry, washing dishes, morning coffee
  on the balcony — listening not to cry but because his voice is something precious saved from a fire. Grief still
  arrives without warning — pouring tea into a second cup, waking thinking he called her. Weeks pass. One afternoon she
  throws all the flowers into the bin and stands looking at the empty vases. That night she cleans the room, changes the
  sheets, opens the windows, washes the "too thin" blanket he always complained about. For months she slept on his side;
  that night she moves back to her own: "I'm trying to move on." Next morning she wakes before the alarm feeling unusually
  heavy; nausea; she thinks it's grief (the night before she looked at old photos, cried, fell asleep with a photo on
  her chest, didn't eat). Nausea again after making tea; she stands in the kitchen, hand on her stomach. A thought — she
  shakes her head; she has hoped before, every late period, every strange morning, always ended by a test or a doctor's
  sympathetic silence; not again, especially without Eethan. In the afternoon nausea again; Luha: "What's wrong?" "Nothing."
  "You always say that." "Dizzy and nauseous." "Since when?" "Maybe a month." "Amaan, when was your last period?" Luha's
  face changes. "I don't know, I don't track dates" — since he died she can't count time. "Did you take a test?" Luha
  sits beside her; she shakes her head: "It won't be that. I can't bear that pain again." "You don't have to hope. Just
  take a test." Luha holds her hand; Amaan looks at their joined hands. A pregnancy test is a small plastic thing, but she
  knows the weight it carries. "What if it's negative?" "Then we'll face it." "What if it's positive?" "We'll face that
  too." Luha's eyes fill. Amaan looks toward the sitting room: under the mirror, a framed photo of Eethan smiling, his hand
  on Amaan's shoulder. The test lies face down on the bathroom counter (show it on a small shelf/counter by a window or in
  her hand — NOT a toilet or bathroom fixtures; a closed bathroom door is fine); she leans on the counter listening to
  Luha in the kitchen; smell of tea and rain. She turns it over: two lines. She can't believe it; waits for the lines to
  vanish; they don't. "Luha?" Her hands tremble. Luha in the doorway; one look at her face and she knows. Amaan only shows
  the test. Luha looks, hand over her mouth. Amaan breaks into sobs; Luha hugs her (sisters, allowed). "Positive. Really
  positive." Five years of waiting, medicines, lost hope — finally a life inside her. The dream Eethan dreamed. She sits
  on the floor holding the test; Luha kneels beside her crying too: "Let's see a doctor soon." Amaan looks at her belly —
  unchanged outside. She rests her palm on it: "Hello." She glances at the door with an innocent hope Eethan will come
  in asking why they're crying; she imagines telling him, his face changing, laughing and crying, worrying at once about
  everything they haven't bought. "I wish Eethan were here." "I know." Luha's hand on her shoulder. "He said he'd come home
  very soon." She presses the test to her chest; Luha has no answer. That night alone in the sitting room she plays his
  last message softly; every word has a new meaning: "if one day we meet a little miracle…" — "We met," she whispers, hand
  on her belly, eyes full. Months later: in the small children's room Eethan had built years ago, Amaan stands with her
  hand on her (now visibly pregnant, modest loose dress) belly; golden late-afternoon rays across a small crib and folded
  baby clothes. She sets the phone by the window and plays his message one last time; his voice fills the quiet room; she
  feels him beside her; the dream they shared, the room, the tiny clothes — she hopes Eethan can see them. "Eethan loved
  you so much, little one." The light fades to gold; one hand on her chest, one on her belly. Eethan is gone, but his
  love stays with her. For the first time since he left, she smiles thinking about her happy future.

## Content rules (BINDING — they override the literal narration)
1. **The accident is never depicted**: no crash, no wrecked car, no skid, no headlights hurtling at the camera, no
   injury, no hospital bed, no body, no funeral bier. The news = two police officers at the door in the rain; grief =
   Amaan on her knees/sitting on the floor, the empty side of the bed, his mug/glasses, rain on the window, flowers.
   The imagined voice-message scene shows Eethan calm in his car at night with rain on the windscreen — never danger.
2. **Married couple (Amaan + Eethan)**: no kissing, no embracing, no lying together, no bedroom scenes with both. They
   may stand or sit side by side, look at each other, hold hands on a table / in the car (close-up of two hands with
   wedding rings is ideal), her hand on his forearm. The back-hug at the stove → standing side by side, her hand on his
   forearm. Forehead/cheek kisses → omitted (show a smile at the door / adjusting a tie at a distance).
3. **No man touches Amaan except Eethan** — the officer catching her as she falls → she sinks to her knees, officers a
   step away. Dr. Mathews only across the desk. Luha (sister) may hug her, hold her hand, put a hand on her shoulder.
4. **Hijab always**: Amaan and Luha (and every woman) wear a hijab fully covering hair and neck in every shot, also at
   home, in bed, and in the honeymoon photo; loose long-sleeved ankle-length clothing; no "hoodie and bare legs", no
   nightclothes (bedtime = her cream dress + a grey cardigan + hijab). Eethan never shirtless.
5. **Bathroom**: never show a toilet, shower or bath; the pregnancy test is a small plain white plastic stick in her hand
   or on a counter by a frosted window, two faint pink lines, no text/logo. Medical items: pill organisers, medicine
   boxes (blank), an ultrasound printout as a soft grey blur — no needles, no injections, no blood.
6. In bed (alone) she is SITTING UP against the headboard, never lying down; or show the empty bed / pillow without her.
7. Clinic waiting room: the pregnant woman is modestly dressed with a hijab; her husband sits beside her, NOT touching.
8. **No readable text anywhere**: phone screens are only a soft glow / blurred chat bubbles / a waveform; no clock digits
   (analog clock faces without numerals); no "LOCAL CALL"; books/baby-names book with blank pages; no logos on clothes,
   no university print. The cover's payphone may appear only as a symbolic image (no text on it).
9. No alcohol, no smoking, no dogs; Amaan "crying when she sees a cat" — a cat may appear in a memory.
10. Faces in the upper two-thirds, calm lower third. Never use the words kiss, hug, embrace, crash, accident, dead,
    death, body, blood, injection, needle, toilet, bathroom, bed (use "the edge of the mattress"/"against the headboard"
    if needed — gen_images strips trigger words on retry anyway) in a `visual` unless unavoidable — describe the safe
    substitute.
