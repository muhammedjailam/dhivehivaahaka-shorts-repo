# Suratul Faatihaa — episode 423 — story notes

## Synopsis
Verses 2–4 of Al-Fatiha and the wisdom of their order. "Lord of the worlds" (majesty, which can make a person feel
small and afraid) is followed at once by "Ar-Rahmanir-Raheem" (mercy) and only then by "Maaliki yawmid-deen".
Mercy: an exhausted mother wakes at night to touch her sick child's forehead; all mothers' tenderness together is not a
drop of Allah's mercy; the hadith of the woman who finds her lost child and clutches him to her chest ("would she throw
her child into the fire?"). A heart hardens when it feels alone in a merciless world, so mercy is recalled twice
(Bismillah and verse 3): majesty alone → despair, mercy alone → carelessness. Your own hardships (loss, illness,
loneliness) may themselves be mercy. Mercy comes first, like a mother who first hugs, then advises; rahma ← rahim
(womb) that shelters the child; mercy builds and prepares before accountability. Verse 4: the Day of Reckoning — awe
but not despair. The powerful, rich and famous will stand utterly alone (no lawyer, money, connections); angels in rows,
Jibreel, no one speaks without permission (an-Naba', al-Infitar). Today's bombed Muslim lands: mothers searching
rubble, a father calling his child, while those who ordered and signed it think there is no reckoning. It concerns all
of us: small daily wrongs (a hurtful word, ignoring, delayed rights, betrayed trust, delaying prayer) will be weighed.
Khawf & rajaa' — two pans of one scale. A long-time sinner wakes up between despair ("no way back" — what Shaytan
loves) and complacency ("I'll enter Paradise whatever I do"), and chooses the middle: the door of repentance is open,
the Day will come, return now. Maalik (Owner) / Malik (King); Pharaoh and Nimrod; today's "my land, my decision, my
world". Ends mid-metaphor: the world is an exam hall — separate desks, own paper, the invigilator doesn't interfere —
but when the exam ends every paper is collected (continues in 424).

## Tone
Reflective and tender in the first half (gold dawn, amber lamps), solemn white-gold awe for the Day, grey-amber grief
for the ruins, cold steel for the powerful, then hope (mosque door at dawn) and a quiet, clear ending in the exam hall.
